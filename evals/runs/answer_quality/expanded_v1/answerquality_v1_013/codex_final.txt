I would treat this as a cash-flow offer, not just a conversion offer.

The good part: six months prepaid can pull cash forward and may improve 30-day economics. In your current snapshot, refills are $55/month with about $28 monthly gross profit, so implied fulfillment/product cost is about $27 per month. Six paid months brings in $330. Before any bonus months, that is roughly $168 gross profit. If you add one bonus month and must fund inventory early, gross profit drops to about $141. With two bonus months, it drops to about $114.

That still clears your $48 CAC on paper, but the constraint is timing: cash collected today becomes an obligation to ship future product. The Money Models warning on Buy X Get Y Free is that prepaid duration offers can work, but only if you can manage the cash and deliver on the promise; they should not be used as survival cash if fulfilling later would strain the business [buy-x-get-y:5]. The continuity material also frames bulk prepaid discounts as a way to boost 30-day profit, but that only helps if the upfront cash is not immediately consumed by early fulfillment obligations [continuity-bonus:9].

Before launching it, I would check five things:

1. Inventory cash reserve: calculate the cash that must be held back for every prepaid customer. At your current numbers, reserve about $27 per refill month promised, including bonus months. Do not spend that reserve on ads or operating expenses.

2. Maximum exposure: cap the test. The source material recommends capping prepaid offers for recurring customers rather than converting the whole base at once [buy-x-get-y:5]. I would start with existing subscribers or high-intent repeat buyers, not cold traffic.

3. Bonus size: one bonus month is much safer than an aggressive “buy six, get six” structure. The larger the bonus, the more it improves conversion but the more it converts cash into future inventory liability.

4. Payback impact: compare the offer’s immediate gross profit after reserved inventory against CAC. If you sell it to new customers, the offer needs to comfortably cover CAC and leave enough cash for fulfillment. If you sell it to existing customers, it is more of a retention and cash-smoothing move.

5. Operational promise: confirm supplier lead times, fulfillment capacity, shelf life, and refund policy. A prepaid refill offer can damage trust if customers feel locked in while shipments slip.

My practical recommendation: run a small “buy six months, get one bonus month” pilot to existing refill customers only. Put the inventory reserve in a separate account, cap the number sold, and judge it by cash left after reserved COGS, not by headline revenue.
